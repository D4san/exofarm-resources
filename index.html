01

The question: can agriculture modify an exoplanet atmosphere?

Agriculture is a sustained way of reorganizing the flow of matter and energy through a biosphere. On Earth, fertilizer production and application have profoundly altered the nitrogen cycle, creating changes in the emissions of ammonia (NH₃) and nitrous oxide (N₂O). ExoFarm asks whether a comparable perturbation on an inhabited planet could alter atmospheric composition enough to become observable from astronomical distances.

The central challenge is attribution. A molecular feature may be detectable without identifying its source, and a large N₂O abundance is not uniquely agricultural: planetary environments can sustain substantial biological or abiotic contributions. ExoFarm therefore represents a proposed atmospheric technosignature that must be evaluated alongside natural nitrogen cycling, stellar irradiation, and other possible atmospheric histories.

02

From planetary nitrogen cycling to atmospheric gases

Nitrogen is essential to known life, but the most abundant atmospheric form, N₂, is not directly usable by most organisms. Fixation converts it into reactive forms that enter biological pathways. Nitrification, denitrification, assimilation, deposition, and other transformations then redistribute nitrogen through soils, waters, organisms, and the atmosphere. Industrial nitrogen fixation increases the amount of reactive nitrogen entering these networks.

NH₃ and N₂O do not behave as equivalent tracers. Ammonia is closely tied to reduced nitrogen and surface exchange; its atmospheric abundance is strongly limited by dissolution, deposition, aerosol formation, and chemical destruction. Nitrous oxide largely reflects microbial nitrogen transformations and persists much longer after emission, allowing it to integrate distributed fluxes across a planetary environment. Understanding both signals therefore requires following their sources and sinks—not assigning an atmospheric abundance directly to agricultural intensity.

03

Agricultural scenarios and stellar environments

Four scenarios represent the progression from a pre-agricultural baseline (A0) to modern Earth (A1), a moderate perturbation (A2), and an extreme ExoFarm state (A3). Instead of imposing a final concentration, the study scales lower-boundary surface emission fluxes of NH₃ and N₂O between the terrestrial reference levels. The moderate scenario uses scaling coefficients of 3.50 for NH₃ and 2.55 for N₂O, while A3 uses 15 for both species.

The surface fluxes are propagated through VULCAN, a one-dimensional photochemical model, in atmospheric environments irradiated by the Sun, TRAPPIST-1, and Proxima Centauri. Pressure–temperature structures and vertical mixing profiles are held fixed within each simulated environment to isolate chemical responses to the agricultural forcing. Consequently, this analysis tests the effects of imposed atmospheric boundary conditions rather than predicting agricultural emissions from population, crops, climate, or land use.

04

Why the host star changes the result

Stellar irradiation controls photolysis and the radicals involved in atmospheric destruction pathways. The same emission flux can therefore generate different molecular abundances and vertical distributions around different stars. This matters especially for M dwarfs, whose ultraviolet spectral energy distributions are unlike the Sun's and can alter the production and removal of nitrogen-bearing species.

Across the adopted stellar spectra, the simulations show that the enhanced NH₃ abundance can persist over a much broader altitude range under M-dwarf irradiation. N₂O generally tracks changes in its imposed surface flux within each environment, but its column abundance and vertical distribution remain stellar dependent. These modeled atmospheric responses should not be interpreted as a universal prediction for every M dwarf, since the adopted UV fields and surface sinks are important assumptions.

05

Connecting chemistry to simulated observations

The photochemical abundance profiles are propagated into synthetic spectra with POSEIDON. A TRAPPIST-1e analogue is considered in transmission, using instrumental uncertainty estimates from PandExo for a JWST observing configuration. A separate Earth-like atmosphere under Proxima Centauri irradiation is used for thermal-emission spectra, with detectability estimates for the LIFE mission concept generated through LIFEsim.

A convenient first sensitivity diagnostic compares spectral contributions from enhanced molecules against model spectra where a selected molecule is returned to the pre-agricultural profile. These residuals help identify favorable wavelengths and observing conditions. However, a molecular signal-to-noise ratio is not itself evidence that the molecule could be unambiguously recovered: overlapping opacity and atmospheric degeneracies require a retrieval analysis.

06

Molecular evidence and atmospheric enrichment

For the simulated LIFE observations, the study uses POSEIDON with MultiNest to perform Bayesian atmospheric retrievals for the modern-Earth (A1) and extreme ExoFarm (A3) scenarios at 1, 10, and 100 hours. Molecular evidence is assessed by comparing the full atmospheric model with models that omit one molecular absorber while allowing other model parameters to readjust. The reported ln B values are conditional on the adopted observational configuration and retrieval parameterization.

Under A3, the evidence for N₂O is strong by 10 hours (ln B = 9.142 ± 0.292), while NH₃ attains moderate molecular evidence at 100 hours (ln B = 2.749 ± 0.345). In A1 at 100 hours, N₂O also receives positive molecular evidence (ln B = 9.834 ± 0.341): identifying a molecule is therefore distinct from demonstrating that its abundance exceeds an agricultural reference state. The retrievals also support major atmospheric molecules such as H₂O, CO₂, and CH₄ and constrain aspects of the planetary atmospheric context.

A separate enrichment calculation compares the retrieved abundances against the modern-Earth A1 benchmark. For the extreme ExoFarm case at 100 hours, the joint posterior probability that both N₂O and NH₃ exceed their A1 reference levels reaches 98.67% under the model assumptions. This demonstrates the potential to infer an intensified nitrogen-cycle state, but does not uniquely identify an agricultural origin.

07

Limits, interpretation, and future work

The scenarios prescribe NH₃ and N₂O surface emissions without a coupled biogeochemical model connecting farming practices to those fluxes. Fixed atmospheric thermal structures and eddy mixing, simplified instrumental noise, and cloud-free synthetic spectra introduce further limitations. Simulated chemical responses are sensitive to the assumed stellar UV spectra, their discretization, and surface sinks—particularly important for ammonia.

The study therefore defines a route from surface forcing to observable atmospheric consequences, rather than a definitive test for extraterrestrial agriculture. A stronger future assessment would combine photochemistry and transport with coupled surface biogeochemistry, multiple plausible natural N₂O and NH₃ sources, varying stellar spectra, clouds, and retrievals that infer surface emission fluxes. Those developments are needed to assess whether atmospheric enrichment is consistent with intensive agriculture or could arise without it.
